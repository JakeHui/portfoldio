Legobot research

Legobot is the endovascular surgical robot I worked on at the Biomechatronics & Intelligent Robotics Lab from June to September 2026. My contribution focused on CAD and mechanical prototyping for catheter and guidewire manipulation, especially the internal coaxial roller assemblies.

The design review centered on a practical problem: transmitting motor motion to a slender instrument while limiting slip, friction, backlash, and unwanted axial drift.

July roller assembly redesign

During the week of July 13, I redesigned the bottom assembly for the internal coaxial rollers and reorganized the imported CAD into a single module with clearly organized components.

  • Added another set of rollers to improve grip and distribute contact.
  • Changed the roller structure into two mating parts with more screw connections, aiming to improve stiffness and assembly strength.
  • Integrated a wire guide between the rollers to support alignment through the mechanism.
  • Refined the roller clearances to reduce unwanted rubbing and friction.

These were completed CAD changes. Their intended benefits—better grip, alignment, and mechanical strength—still require quantitative comparison with the earlier design.

Design review and tradeoffs

My review identified slippage during instrument rotation, printed-part friction, and a wobbling coupler bevel gear as priorities. I also examined screw access, the reliability of threads cut directly into PLA, and whether parts could be replaced without dismantling the entire mechanism.

The notes compare nominal torque and roller-force estimates with observed slipping. I used that gap to focus the design review on contact geometry and clamping rather than treating motor torque alone as a measure of usable insertion force.

Other questions included how to set a repeatable clamping force, keep the motor’s rotating shell clear of the printed housing, and reduce sensitivity to print orientation and dimensional variation.

Proposed next iterations

  • Compare V-shaped rollers and different rubber hardnesses for grip and instrument alignment.
  • Evaluate a differential drive arrangement for coupled motor actuation, accounting for backlash, transmission losses, and control complexity.
  • Consider off-the-shelf shafts, D-profile axles, heat-set inserts, and more accessible fasteners to improve repeatability and serviceability.
  • Explore force or torque feedback and a dedicated controller after addressing the mechanical limitations.

These are design directions from my research notes, not validated performance improvements. Questions about instrument limits, sterilization, detachable modules, and steerable-catheter control remained part of the design review.
