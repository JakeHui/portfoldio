Research question and platform

My research at NYU Prema Lab uses a modular robotic fish to investigate how tail mechanics influence underwater locomotion. I began by reviewing swimming modes, body stiffness, tail geometry, and modular fish designs, then built on the existing ScaFish mechanism.

An early hand-driven scale replica used a TPU tail and movable rib locations to study the crank-and-tendon mechanism before integrating powered actuation. Modularity makes it possible to change components and examine their influence on tail motion.

CAD revisions and fabrication

In the week of July 13, I renewed the fish CAD around actuator compatibility, rigidity, and assembly. A taller drive gear accommodates motors mounted at different heights without changing the central crankshaft geometry. Additional mounting holes and a central crankshaft support stiffen the mechanism, while a slotted head connection with more fasteners makes the head easier to interchange and secure.

The July 20 build notes record an assembled fish and several practical issues: consistent string tension, torsion in the printed axle, the motor mount, and the acrylic front section. These observations guided the next fabrication steps and tensioning work.

Actuation and electronics

I develop Python routines for Dynamixel servo control and visualize tail position and motor velocity during testing. The build work also explored small-servo testing and waterproofing, alongside the Dynamixel-based approach.

The electronics plan includes an ESP32, a Dynamixel interface, battery power, and an enclosure positioned with weight distribution in mind. Wireless control, tracking, and a learned control policy are development goals rather than demonstrated autonomous capabilities.

Tail stiffness and water testing

Bench and pool tests connect the CAD changes to the fish’s motion. During one water test, I observed that the tail had little deflection beyond the tendon crossover point: it formed more of a C shape than the desired S-shaped motion, with substantial rocking.

I suspected excessive stiffness in the nitinol reinforcement. Removing some wire improved the swimming behavior, which motivated a closer comparison with the glass-fiber rods used in the older ScaFish. This remains a working explanation from testing, not a completed material-performance study.

Next research steps

The next priorities are repeatable tendon tension, calibrated straight swimming and turning, electronics integration, and tracking of the robot’s motion. A longer-term goal is to collect useful motion data and demonstrate a control policy.

Additional ideas from the literature review include changing tail stiffness and geometry, movable deflection points, individually actuated pectoral fins, and buoyancy control. These remain candidate experiments; the current work focuses on making the existing platform repeatable and measurable.
