Published research and objective

My published research investigates a coaxial swerve drivetrain for wheelchair mobility. The objective is lateral and diagonal movement without first turning the chair, potentially making confined spaces easier to navigate.

The work combines a review of existing drivetrains, CAD development, prototype construction, control design, and a small comparative maneuverability test. It was completed through the Lumiere Research Program and published in the Curieux Academic Journal.

Read the published research

Coaxial swerve module design

Each wheel is independently driven and steered. Coaxial shafts and bevel gears transmit drive motion while allowing the module to rotate about its steering axis.

I modeled a simplified module that removes an additional gear stage used in some earlier arrangements. The design intent was to reduce bulk, mass, cost, and the number of potential failure points while keeping the module suitable for mounting to a larger chassis.

Control architecture

The paper describes a loop that reads joystick commands and wheel encoder angles, converts the requested translation and rotation into individual wheel states using swerve kinematics, and sends speed and steering commands to each module.

The proposed implementation uses a Raspberry Pi and an Adafruit motor-control HAT. Separate joystick inputs command translation and rotation, allowing direction of travel and orientation to be controlled independently.

Prototype comparison

The obstacle-course test compared the same prototype in two modes: constrained non-holonomic motion and full holonomic motion. Each mode was run three times.

Obstacle-course times reported in the paper
ModeTrial timesReported average
Constrained motion8.56, 9.92, 9.23 s9.24 s
Holonomic motion5.84, 6.35, 5.27 s5.88 s

The paper also reports an approximately 34 cm turning radius with the center of rotation at the chassis center, compared with 53 cm when positioned between the rear wheels. Prototype maximum speed was approximately 0.5 m/s.

Limitations and future work

These results come from a small prototype study in one test environment. The paper identifies sample size, operator effects, measurement error, and prototype limitations as reasons not to generalize the results to everyday wheelchair use.

Further work includes energy-consumption measurements, improved speed and power, long-term durability, stability on different surfaces, broader user feedback, and pre-programmed path following. The study establishes a design direction rather than a clinically validated mobility product.
